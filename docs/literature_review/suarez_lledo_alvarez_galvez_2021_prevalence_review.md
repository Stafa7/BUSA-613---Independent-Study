# Suarez-Lledo and Alvarez-Galvez 2021 - Prevalence of Health Misinformation on Social Media

## Citation

Suarez-Lledo, V., & Alvarez-Galvez, J. (2021). *Prevalence of Health Misinformation on Social Media: Systematic Review*. Journal of Medical Internet Research, 23(1), e17187. https://doi.org/10.2196/17187

## Purpose

This systematic review examines how prevalent health misinformation is across social media platforms and health topics. The authors argue that there is broad agreement that health misinformation is a serious problem, but less agreement about its magnitude, topical distribution, and platform-specific prevalence. The paper's central contribution is an organized measurement-oriented review of health misinformation prevalence, rather than a general discussion of misinformation harms.

The paper defines health misinformation broadly as a health-related claim that is based on anecdotal evidence, false, or misleading because of lack of scientific knowledge. This definition includes misinformation, disinformation, and malinformation under a wider practical umbrella because intent is often difficult to determine.

## Methodology

The review follows PRISMA guidelines. The authors searched PubMed, MEDLINE, Scopus, and Web of Science for English-language articles published before March 2019. The search was organized around three dimensions: social media, health, and misinformation. Studies were included when they focused on health misinformation on social media, discussed the impact, consequences, or purposes of misinformation, and reported empirical results through quantitative, qualitative, or computational methods.

The final sample included 69 studies. For each study, the authors coded the health topic, platform, method, misinformation prevalence, and methodological quality. The most common platforms were Twitter, YouTube, and Facebook, with Twitter dominating the literature.

## Main Findings

The authors identify six principal categories of health misinformation research:

- vaccines: 32% of reviewed studies,
- drugs or smoking: 22%,
- noncommunicable diseases: 19%,
- pandemics: 10%,
- eating disorders: 9%,
- medical treatments: 7%.

The methodological approaches were also grouped into five major categories:

- social network analysis: 28%,
- evaluating content: 26%,
- evaluating quality: 24%,
- content/text analysis: 16%,
- sentiment analysis: 6%.

Health misinformation was most prevalent in studies related to smoking products and drugs, including opioids and marijuana; in some studies, misinformation reached 87% of posts. Vaccine misinformation was also common, with the paper reporting an average prevalence around 43%, and HPV vaccine misinformation receiving notable attention. Misinformation around diets and pro-eating-disorder arguments was also substantial, while disease-related misinformation, including cancer and pandemics, was moderate but still important. Medical-treatment misinformation had the lowest average prevalence among the reviewed categories, though the authors caution that this area had less evidence overall.

The paper also emphasizes that misinformation prevalence differs by platform. Microblogging platforms such as Twitter are especially visible in the literature and are important sites for misinformation about vaccines, drugs, and smoking products. Media-sharing platforms such as YouTube are often used to assess content quality around health topics, treatments, and disease information.

## Limitations

The review predates the COVID-19 pandemic because the search covered literature before March 2019. Therefore, it does not include the pandemic-era infodemic literature, CoAID, or many transformer-era detection studies. The authors also note conceptual variation in definitions of health misinformation, variation in measurement techniques, and potential platform/data-access biases. Twitter is heavily represented partly because it has historically been easier to access than other platforms.

## Relevance to This Independent Study

This paper is useful for the background and motivation section. It supports the claim that health misinformation is widespread, topic-specific, platform-dependent, and relevant to public-health communication. It also supports the project's move beyond binary fake/real classification because misinformation differs by domain, platform, and form.

For this project, the most useful connection is the paper's emphasis on prevalence, topical categories, and platform differences. These motivate later phases involving narrative discovery, topic modeling, sentiment/emotion analysis, and engagement analytics.
