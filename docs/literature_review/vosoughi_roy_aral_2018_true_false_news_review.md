# Vosoughi, Roy, and Aral 2018 - The Spread of True and False News Online

## Citation

Vosoughi, S., Roy, D., & Aral, S. (2018). *The Spread of True and False News Online*. Science, 359(6380), 1146-1151. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aap9559

## Purpose

This paper is a foundational empirical study of how true and false news diffuse differently online. Although it is not health-specific, it is central for any project analyzing misinformation engagement and diffusion. The authors explicitly avoid the term "fake news" because it has become politicized and imprecise. They instead use true and false news, based on veracity assessments by fact-checking organizations.

## Data and Methods

The authors analyze all fact-checked true and false rumor cascades that spread on Twitter from 2006 to 2017. The data include about 126,000 stories tweeted by about 3 million people more than 4.5 million times. Stories were classified using six independent fact-checking organizations: Snopes, PolitiFact, FactCheck.org, TruthOrFiction, Hoax-Slayer, and Urban Legends. The organizations showed 95% to 98% agreement on classifications.

The authors define news broadly as a story or claim with an assertion. A rumor is the social phenomenon of that claim spreading through Twitter. A rumor cascade begins when a user tweets a claim and others propagate it through retweets. The study measures diffusion through depth, size, maximum breadth, and structural virality.

The paper also examines novelty, emotional responses in replies, and the role of bots.

## Main Findings

The core finding is that falsehood diffuses farther, faster, deeper, and more broadly than truth across all information categories. False political news shows the strongest effect, but differences also appear in other categories such as science, urban legends, terrorism, natural disasters, entertainment, business, and financial information.

False news reaches more people at every cascade depth. The paper reports that truth rarely diffuses to more than 1,000 people, while the top 1% of false-news cascades routinely diffuse to between 1,000 and 100,000 people. Falsehood also reaches 1,500 people about six times faster than truth and reaches deep cascade levels much faster.

The authors find that false news is more novel than true news, suggesting that novelty contributes to sharing behavior. Emotional responses also differ: false stories inspire fear, disgust, and surprise, while true stories inspire anticipation, sadness, joy, and trust.

Contrary to common assumptions, bots do not explain the difference. Bots accelerate both true and false news at similar rates. The authors therefore conclude that false news spreads more than truth because humans are more likely to spread it.

## Limitations

The study is not health-specific, and it covers Twitter from 2006 to 2017. Platform design, moderation, and user behavior have changed since then. The study also depends on stories that were checked by fact-checking organizations, which may not represent all misinformation.

## Relevance to This Independent Study

This paper supports the social engagement analytics part of the project. It provides a strong rationale for comparing text-only models with engagement-informed models: false and true information can have different diffusion profiles. It also motivates sentiment/emotion analysis, since emotional reactions differ between false and true stories.

In the final report, this paper should be used as foundational evidence that misinformation risk includes spread, novelty, emotional response, and human sharing behavior, not only textual falsehood.
